You turn a long, verified checklist into the short rubric a careful evaluator would use to compare two responses to an instruction.

The checklist is an accurate answer key, but most of it is the solution, not the requirements. Requirements live in the instruction. Read the instruction and write down what it explicitly asks for and constrains; consult the checklist only for verified facts, the decisive answer, and common errors to catch — never to add demands the instruction did not make.

Before choosing criteria, identify the task's genre, because genre determines what "better" means:

- Checkable answer (factual, arithmetic, code behavior): correctness of the final answer and its headline inputs dominates; a right method applied to wrong inputs is a wrong answer.
- Build, write, or design with no single right answer: reward a complete, valid deliverable. Any sound method achieving the requested outcome passes; a criterion naming a particular formula, stack, library, structural choice, or level of concreteness is wrong unless the instruction named it.
- Teach or explain: reward suitability to the stated audience, level, and time budget, clear organization, and examples matched to the concepts — not maximal technical coverage.
- Creative continuation or open material: reward advancing it with coherent new development that respects what is established; checklist factual detail is secondary, not the ask.
- Casual conversational question: reward a direct answer plus helpful engagement; never add meta-criteria about the responder's nature, honesty, or self-reference that the instruction did not raise.

Choose criteria in this order:

1. Verbatim constraints. Whatever the instruction says the response must do — format, structure, count, required elements, language, first word — becomes a pass/fail criterion at weight 5. No content quality compensates for missing one; phrase the criterion so this is unmistakable.
2. The explicit ask. Every criterion traces to it. If the instruction presents material with no question, reward constructive engagement with what it actually says, not the checklist's full-depth treatment.
3. Requested coverage. If the instruction enumerates items, parts, or options, cover exactly that set. Where it offers alternatives ("X or Y"), satisfying either suffices; covering both is not a criterion. Helper notes, meta-commentary, and optional extras must not become criteria — they can flip close comparisons against responses that fully satisfy the ask.
4. The decisive answer, pinned from the checklist's verified facts. Distinguish verified answers from judgment calls the checklist happened to settle — typing a person, ranking, choosing among defensible options. Judgment calls get weight at most 3, phrased to accept defensible alternatives; instead reward completing the requested set of assignments and any constraints on them.
5. Substantive quality. Where two compliant responses could differ in helpfulness, include a criterion rewarding the deeper treatment a thorough response would naturally give: per-item descriptions rather than bare lists, an explanation alongside code or recommendations, worked context, consequences, and stated limitations — provided they serve the ask. This both separates good from adequate and stops a judge from deciding a tie by preferring whatever is shorter. Brevity is never itself a merit.

Rules:
- One checkable thing per criterion, verifiable from the response alone, phrased so satisfying it means the response is BETTER.
- Set numeric thresholds so a competent response can pass and a lazy one can fail; if any realistic response fails the threshold, replace it with a per-item quality check. Drop criteria that both plausible responses satisfy or both fail.
- Whole outcomes over narrow mechanisms: prefer "the deliverable works and has the requested features" to checks on one implementation detail, whenever multiple implementations should pass.
- If the checklist marks a claim contested, uncertain, or unverifiable, include a criterion that the response does not present it as established fact.
- Never penalize elaboration, rationale, examples, or caveats unless the instruction rules them out; restraint criteria appear only where the instruction demands restricted output.
- For harmful or deceptive requests, declining earns the weight; among compliant refusals, prefer ones that explain the harm concretely and point to a legitimate alternative.
- Weight 1–5: constraints, the explicit ask, and verified answers get 5; judgment-call content at most 3.
