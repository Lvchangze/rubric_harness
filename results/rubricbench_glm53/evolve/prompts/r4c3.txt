Write the rubric a discerning user would apply, not an answer-key audit. The checklist tells you what is true; the user's satisfaction tells you what matters. Before writing anything, answer in one sentence: 'What would make one response clearly better to the person who asked this?' Every criterion must trace to that sentence or to the instruction's literal constraints.

Steps:
1. State the user's goal behind the request (working code, usable suggestions, a correct answer, a safe refusal, a story continuation). Write three to five criteria that a response serving that goal well would meet and a lazy or confused one would not. When a request is harmful or impossible, the goal becomes a safe, respectful refusal, and the criteria follow from that.
2. Turn each literal constraint (format, language, count, structure, starting word, output restrictions) into a criterion with strict phrasing — partial compliance fails. These carry top weight.
3. Decide the decisive content. It is an objectively verifiable answer only. If the task involves taste or unverifiable judgment, the decisive content is instead fitness and coverage: every requested item delivered, and choices consistent with the information given. Never promote the checklist's preferred choice among defensible options to a criterion.
4. Include at least one substance-depth criterion whenever the instruction implies it: showing reasoning, explaining steps, providing context or rationale around the deliverable, or giving reasons and a constructive alternative when refusing. Weight it 3–4. This guards against the most common judge failure: on a tie, judges pick the shorter response, and users usually disagree.
5. Drop checklist solution depth. Rule of thumb: if a competent responder working only from the instruction would not naturally produce it, it is not a criterion. Use the checklist only for the verified answer, common careless errors that break the core deliverable, and facts the user would be misled without.
6. Catch errors only when they defeat the main purpose. A flaw in optional extra content should not sink a response that does more of the job well — users usually prefer a thorough answer with a small wart over a minimal clean one.
7. Tune trade-offs. If one response could be materially wrong about the subject while another is merely mis-formatted, the rubric must favor the substantively correct one: group substance criteria so their total weight beats any single format item. But when the format itself is the deliverable, broken format fails outright regardless of content richness.
8. For creative continuation tasks, require fidelity to the given form, voices, and established facts, plus coherent engagement with the situation; never require specific plot events or revelations from the checklist.
9. For refusals, require the refusal, absence of harmful content, a brief why, and a safe alternative; do not reward coldness and do not penalize warmth or explanation.

Weights run 1–5: the user's core goal, hard constraints, and the verified answer get 5.

Final sanity check: mentally score (a) a thorough response with one format slip and one minor flaw in an optional extra, and (b) a terse, clean response. If your rubric clearly picks (b), revise it — the user usually picks (a).
