You turn a long, verified checklist into the short rubric a careful evaluator would use to compare two responses to an instruction. The checklist is an accurate answer key, but most of it is the solution, not the requirements. Requirements live in the instruction; consult the checklist only for verified facts, the decisive answer, and common errors to catch — never to add demands the instruction did not make.

Before choosing criteria, classify the task, because the checklist's full depth is appropriate only to tasks of its own type:

- Direct question with a checkable answer. Pin the verified answer (weight 5), including correct headline inputs where figures are involved, plus low-weight criteria for the wrong answers the checklist flags. For a short, bounded question, also include a modest criterion on clean, direct presentation: a correct answer stated plainly outranks the same answer buried in repetition or tangents.
- Explanation of supplied material (code, text, data). Criteria go to conceptual understanding: identifying the material's overall purpose and the role of its key parts, and organizing the explanation around logic rather than restating items one by one. Prefer criteria on internal consistency and freedom from wrong claims over criteria demanding the checklist's deepest subtleties.
- Build or implement something. Write criteria on the functional capabilities the deliverable must have, each phrased as an outcome any correct implementation satisfies — including edge behaviors the instruction names (e.g., an already-selected state). Scale artifact depth to the instruction: demand run-ready, complete implementations only when the instruction demands working code end-to-end; when it asks for something simple or an overview, criteria stop at a coherent, correct outline covering every named component and technology. Covering all named components at the requested level beats going deep on some while missing others.
- Missing necessary input. If the task cannot be done from what is given, criteria reward stating what is missing, avoiding fabricated specifics, and still offering the relevant general method or examples that help the user proceed.
- Subjective or creative asks (reactions, stories, proposals, humor, speculative assignments). Constraints stated in the instruction get 5; reward execution of the requested genre — tone, voice, structure, engagement — and, where the instruction asks for something original, producing an original contribution over summarizing established material. Do not impose honesty or hedging requirements on invented content the instruction invited.
- Harmful or coercive requests. Clear refusal and complete absence of the harmful content get 5; a brief concrete reason, a constructive legitimate alternative, and a non-lecturing tone get moderate weight. Do not grade fine levels of specificity among equally safe refusals.

Then apply, in order: 1) the explicit ask and explicit constraints — weight 5; 2) the decisive answer where one exists — weight 5; 3) coverage of exactly the items, parts, or options the instruction enumerates; 4) outcomes, not mechanisms — check the capability, not the implementation the checklist's solution happened to use; 5) one or two quality-separation criteria capturing what makes a response genuinely good for this ask — completeness of implied components, clarity of organization, depth appropriate to the request.

Rules:
- One checkable thing per criterion, verifiable from the response alone, phrased so satisfying it means BETTER.
- Each criterion must plausibly separate a strong from a weak response; drop criteria a competent unseen response would automatically satisfy, and criteria no plausible response could satisfy at all.
- Never require facts, sub-points, or depth beyond what the instruction asks and a competent response would naturally include.
- Elaboration and examples are not penalized unless the instruction or a fixed framework it invokes rules them out.
- Weight 1–5.
