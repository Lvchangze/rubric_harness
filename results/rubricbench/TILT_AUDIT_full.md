## 2. The null distribution under random re-assignment of the winner

Check 1 as asked: permute which response is the preferred one, keep everything
else fixed, confirm the null sits on zero.

**What this test can establish, stated before running it.** Swapping the winner
and the loser for a case negates that case's contribution, so a label
permutation maps every per-case term `t_i` to `±t_i` with probability one half.
The null distribution of the mean is therefore *exactly* symmetric about zero
by construction, for any source and any tokenisation. Running it confirms the
implementation has no sign or indexing error, and nothing else: in particular it
has no power against a length confound, because a length confound lives entirely
in the winner/loser assignment that the permutation destroys. The nulls with
power over that are in §3.

| source | observed tilt | null mean | null SD | null 2.5% | null 97.5% | share of null > 0 | permutation p |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| `framed` | +0.0013 | +0.00003 | 0.0022 | -0.0043 | +0.0043 | 0.504 | 0.5474 |
| `baseline` | -0.0019 | +0.00000 | 0.0021 | -0.0042 | +0.0042 | 0.501 | 0.3655 |
| `expert` | +0.0193 | +0.00002 | 0.0029 | -0.0057 | +0.0057 | 0.505 | 0 ** |

20,000 sign-flip permutations, seed 20260831. The null means are zero to four
decimals and the exceedance rate above zero is 0.50 in every row, as the
symmetry argument requires. **Check 1 passes and tells us the statistic is not
mechanically biased.** It does not yet distinguish rubric content from response
asymmetry.

### 3.4 Two nulls that keep the response pair and break the rubric's link to it

A length confound cannot be tested by permuting labels (§2). It can be tested
by keeping the pair exactly as it is and replacing the rubric with something
that cannot know anything about it:

- **frequency-matched random tokens** — each rubric token is swapped for a
  random token drawn from the same document-frequency band over the 2,294
  responses, rejecting draws that occur in the case's own instruction. Same
  number of tokens, same rarity profile, no connection to the pair.
- **another case's real expert rubric** — the rubric of a different case in the

### 3.5 The same question with the nuisance removed entirely

Tilt's denominator is every rubric token, most of which either hit both
responses or neither and carry no information. Restrict attention to the tokens

involve the pair, that share is 0.5, regardless of length, vocabulary or
response count, so this version needs no null at all.

## 6. Does `expert` actually win more where the tilt is higher?

The confirmatory test. If tilt is a real mechanism — pair-specific information
in the rubric, passed to the judge — then it should show a dose effect: the
cases where the expert rubric leans hardest toward the winning response should
be the cases where `expert` beats a rubric that cannot lean at all. If instead
tilt is a nuisance correlate of "this pair is lexically easy", it will predict
*every* source's accuracy about equally, including sources with zero tilt.

### 6.4 How many of the 16 points the tilt is worth

The claim under audit is not "tilt exists" but "tilt makes part of the
`expert`-minus-`framed` gap unreachable". That is a statement about a product:
how much accuracy one unit of tilt buys, times how much tilt `expert` has. Three
ways of estimating it, all on the paired outcome (`expert` correct − `framed`
correct) so that case difficulty cancels.

| predictor | mean | slope of the gap on it | 95% CI | attributable to it | 95% CI |
|---|--:|--:|:--:|--:|:--:|
| `expert` tilt | +0.0193 | +0.2507 | [-0.0037, +0.5158] | **+0.0049** | [-0.0001, +0.0102] |
| `expert` excess tilt | +0.0191 | +0.2559 | [-0.0140, +0.5432] | **+0.0049** | [-0.0003, +0.0107] |
| `expert` tilt − `framed` tilt (paired) | +0.0181 | +0.3640 | [+0.0901, +0.6611] | **+0.0066** | [+0.0016, +0.0122] |

Observed overall gap on these 1141 cases: **+0.1534**. Fitted gap at
zero tilt: **+0.1485**.

And directly, without a model: on the **324 cases whose excess tilt is within ±0.02 of zero** — where the expert rubric demonstrably leans neither way — `none` scores 0.6049, `framed` 0.6574, `expert` 0.7963. The `expert`-minus-`framed` gap there is **+0.1389**, against +0.1534 over all 1141.

A fourth estimate, deliberately the most generous. The steepest tilt-to-accuracy
conversion anywhere in this data is `framed`'s own from §6.3: +2.061 logits per
unit of tilt, significant at p=0.014. Read as causal and
evaluated at `framed`'s accuracy (0.627, where one logit is worth
0.2340 of probability), `expert`'s mean tilt of +0.0193 would
be worth **+0.0093** of accuracy.

## 7. Leakage, or an annotator who understands the task better?

Both produce a positive tilt and they mean opposite things. "Leakage": the
annotator read the winning response and wrote criteria describing it, so
`expert`'s score is partly the answer key. "Expertise": the annotator read only
the instruction, inferred correctly what a good answer must contain, and the
winning response contains it *because it is the better response* — which is not
leakage at all, it is what a good rubric is supposed to do. Six tests that bear
on the difference, starting with the one that would settle it.

### 7.1 The decisive design, if the benchmark supplies it: one instruction, two pairs

If the same instruction appears in two cases with different response pairs, then
anything derived strictly from the instruction must be the same for both, while
anything read off the responses can differ. Whether that design exists here is a
property of the data:

- distinct normalised instructions among the 1141 audited cases: **1120**
- instructions shared by more than one case: **7**, covering 28 cases
- of the shared-instruction groups, **0** have an identical expert rubric across their cases and **7** do not
- on those cases: own expert rubric tilt **-0.0015** (n=28), sibling's expert rubric on the same instruction **-0.0008** (n=252)

### 7.2 Verbatim phrase overlap by phrase length

A single shared word can be coincidence or shared topic. A shared five-word
phrase between a rubric and one specific response, absent from the instruction,
is very hard to produce without having read that response. For each n, the
rubric's n-grams that do not appear in the instruction are looked up in each
response.

The column to read is the **relative** lift, winner share divided by loser share.
Transcription from the winning response would make it climb with n: a rubric
copied from a response shares long phrases with it and only short ones with the
other. Shared topical vocabulary makes it flat.

### 7.5 Positive and negative criteria separately

A criterion naming what the winner does should tilt positive; one naming the
mistake the loser makes should tilt *negative*, because the error words are in
the loser. Either sign is pair-specific knowledge, so this is a check on what
kind of knowledge it is, not on whether there is any.
